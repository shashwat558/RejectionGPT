# Why Indian Students and Freshers Struggle to Land Tech Jobs, and What Would Fix It

*Research compiled 19 September 2026. Focus: India, entry-level tech roles (software, data, AI/ML, IT services, product companies, GCCs).*

---

## 0. How to read this report

Sources vary a lot in quality. Each problem below is tagged:

- **[Strong]**: backed by a large survey, government data, or a named company/study.
- **[Moderate]**: reported by reputable media or industry bodies, but the sample is narrow, the numbers conflict, or it is partly opinion.
- **[Anecdotal]**: repeated widely (blogs, forums, vendor content) but I could not trace it to solid primary data. Treat as a hypothesis.

Several popular statistics turned out to be weak, and I call those out rather than repeating them.

---

## 1. Executive summary

1. **The problem is not one problem.** It is three layers stacked on top of each other: (a) fewer entry-level slots at the biggest recruiters, (b) a gap between what colleges teach and what employers now test, and (c) preventable candidate-side gaps in awareness, practice, resume, and communication.
2. **The market is mixed, not collapsed.** Naukri reports fresher hiring up 15% year on year in August 2026, while TCS's fresher offers for FY27 started at 25,000 versus 44,000 hired in FY26, and one staffing firm reports a steep fall in tech openings for people with up to two years' experience. Which number you see depends on who is counting and which sector.
3. **AI is changing what "job-ready" means, but is not the only cause.** ICRIER's survey of about 650 IT firms found entry-level hiring slowing, yet the authors say this cannot be pinned on AI alone. AI/ML hiring is growing fast but is skewed toward experienced people, so it is not an easy door for freshers.
4. **Most fixable failures are process failures**: unfocused preparation, resumes that do not show proof of skill, weak communication, and no feedback loop. These are the highest-leverage things a student controls.
5. **The ecosystem preys on desperation**: fake offers with "processing fees" and expensive pay-after-placement courses.
6. **Proposed solution (Section 6):** a *Proof-of-Skill Pathway*, meaning diagnose early, follow a structured plan, practise with feedback, build verifiable proof, and apply through trusted channels. It comes with a student action plan, a college programme, and an employer/policy layer.

**Reading these together:** the total number of graduates is not the whole story. Employers say they cannot find the right skills (AI skills topped the hardest-to-find list in the India Skills Report), while a large pool of freshers cannot get shortlisted. That points to a *matching and readiness* problem as much as a *volume* problem. The employability test measures baseline skills, though, so a high score does not mean high hiring odds.

**Warning on old statistics.** Figures like "only 3% of engineers are fit for software product roles" (Aspiring Minds, 2013 batch) and "18% employable" (2016) are still recycled in articles. They are more than a decade old, and the labour market and tests have changed. I use them only as history.

## 3. The problems, in detail

### A. Market and structural problems (outside the student's control)

**A1. Fewer entry-level slots at the largest recruiters [Strong]**
IT services firms historically absorbed most freshers in bulk. TCS's FY27 offers started well below last year's intake, and the CEO said further hiring depends on demand clarity. ICRIER found more firms cutting than adding entry-level roles. Fresher training is costly (TCS cites up to nine months), so when demand is uncertain, freshers are the first line to be trimmed.

**A2. The bar is rising, not just the competition [Moderate]**
A TeamLease EdTech analysis of 1,000+ employers describes "job-ready" shifting toward comfort with tools, familiarity with real work settings, and fast learning. By 2026, employers also want to see judgement about *when not to rely on AI*: checking outputs and validating results. TCS's CHRO reportedly said about 60% of its FY26 fresher hires were AI-skilled, versus 10–15% three years earlier.

**A3. AI/ML boom is not a fresher boom [Strong]**
AI/ML job postings are growing 25–35% year on year, but Naukri's data shows the growth concentrated among candidates with 8 to 16+ years' experience. A fresher who "does an AI course" is competing for a narrow entry band.

**A4. Boom-bust history [Moderate]**
Campus placements in the 2024 cycle were described by placement officers as the worst in roughly a decade, with policies such as "one student, one job" spreading even to top institutes. Cyclical downturns hit tier-2/3 colleges hardest because they rely on a few mass recruiters.

**A5. Uneven geography [Moderate]**
Hiring growth is strong in Hyderabad, Chennai, Kolkata, Bengaluru, and some emerging cities (Bhubaneswar, Raipur, Surat, Ranchi appear in Naukri data), but students in small towns often lack awareness of, or access to, these hubs.

### B. Education and skill gaps

**B1. Theory-heavy curriculum, slow to update [Moderate]**
Widely reported: many programmes emphasise exams over building working software, and syllabi lag industry tools (cloud, DevOps, modern frameworks, applied AI). Evidence is mostly commentary rather than audited curriculum studies, but the direction is consistent across employers, HR firms, and edtech reports.

**B2. Employability varies sharply by stream and institution [Strong]**
CS/IT graduates score around 78–80% on the employability test, while polytechnic graduates score about 33%. The average hides a wide spread; a student's stream and college shape both skills and access to recruiters.

**B3. Little structured industry exposure [Moderate]**
About 92.8% of students say they want internships (India Skills Report), which signals demand exceeds supply of *good* internships. AICTE is reported to have added tens of thousands of internship openings in 2026 (stipends about ₹8,000–25,000/month), but quality and awareness vary.

**B4. Fundamentals are thin [Moderate]**
Employers repeatedly point to weak grounding in problem-solving, data structures, databases, operating systems, and networks. Expect this to matter more now that AI can generate boilerplate code: employers increasingly test *understanding*, not typing speed.

### C. Candidate-side problems (the ones the student can fix)

**C1. Low awareness [Moderate]**
Students often do not know: which roles exist beyond "software developer" (QA, data analyst, DevOps, support engineering, cloud, cybersecurity, product/business analyst); how different recruiters hire (mass recruiters test aptitude and basic coding; product companies test DSA and design; startups test projects); what stages a hiring funnel has; and which hubs and off-campus channels exist. Many begin serious preparation only in the final year.

**C2. Too little *deliberate* practice [Moderate / Anecdotal on the exact numbers]**
Common pattern: watching tutorials without solving problems under time limits; solving problems but never explaining them aloud; skipping aptitude/reasoning, which is the first filter in mass recruitment; and no mock interviews. Vendor blogs claim figures like "60% of fresher rejections come from basic interview mistakes". I could not verify those numbers, but the qualitative pattern (unclear self-introduction, weak problem explanation, no company research) is consistent across recruiter accounts.

**C3. Resume and profile problems [Moderate]**
The best real dataset I found is GoodSpace's analysis of 33,775 resumes (scanned Mar 2025–Feb 2026 through its own free checker, so it is a self-selected sample):
- average ATS-readiness score 64.8/100; only 0.04% scored above 80;
- 92.5% used dense paragraph blocks instead of bullets;
- the average resume was about 449 words, shorter than the range the report calls ideal.

Other frequent issues (from recruiters and resume-coaching sources): generic objective statements, fancy multi-column templates, missing keywords from the job description, typos in contact details, dead GitHub links, and projects described by tools used instead of *problem solved and result*.

*Caveat:* the claim that "75% of resumes are rejected by ATS before a human sees them" appears everywhere in vendor content but I could not trace it to a primary study. In practice, many ATS tools are mainly databases and search filters, and recruiters or automated screens do the filtering. The safe takeaway is: **make the resume easy to parse and easy to skim in seconds**, not that a robot secretly deletes most applications.

**C4. Projects without proof [Moderate]**
Tutorial-clone projects look identical across thousands of resumes. Employers cannot tell what the student built, decided, or debugged. Few students show deployed apps, tests, README files, or commit history.

**C5. Communication and behavioural readiness [Moderate]**
Employer surveys repeatedly list communication and clarity as gaps (the older HackerEarth piece, the Amar Ujala and Taggd/PlacementPreparation content). Students often can code but cannot explain their approach, trade-offs, or project decisions, which is exactly what live follow-up interviews probe.

**C6. Misusing AI [Moderate]**
Recruiters told Times of India (July 2026) that GenAI-assisted cheating in campus hiring is a growing problem (hidden devices, proxies, AI-generated answers), and companies are responding with AI proctoring and live follow-up interviews. Students who lean on AI to produce code they cannot explain get exposed at the follow-up stage, and it also degrades trust for honest candidates.

**C7. Narrow targeting and mass applying [Anecdotal]**
Sending the same resume to hundreds of listings, ignoring referrals, and applying only to a handful of famous names. Pair this with A1 and the odds worsen.

### D. Hiring-process problems

**D1. Opaque, slow, AI-assisted screening [Moderate]**
Global data (not India-specific): Criteria Corp's 2026 report found 53% of job seekers were ghosted by an employer in the past year, and a PeopleScout survey found candidates who suspect AI screening are far less confident a human reviewed their application. Average time to hire runs about 41 days. Indian freshers report the same silence, so assume it applies but note the data is global.

**D2. Ghost and duplicate postings [Moderate]**
A 2024 survey reported by Fortune found that a large share of recruiters admit posting roles that do not exist or are already filled. Students waste effort on listings that were never open.

**D3. Multi-stage funnels with different skill sets [Moderate]**
Aptitude test, coding round, technical interview, HR round. A student strong in one stage but unprepared for another gets filtered out, and most never learn which stage failed because there is no feedback.

**D4. Long onboarding waits [Historical]**
In the 2022–23 slowdown, freshers at large IT firms waited many months after offer letters before joining. This is a recurring risk in downturns rather than a current headline, but students should ask about joining timelines.

### E. Ecosystem risks

**E1. Fake job offers and "processing fee" scams [Strong on existence]**
SBI issued a public warning in 2024 about fake offers via WhatsApp, SMS, and email; HRKatha documented cases like a ₹9,000 "processing fee". Scams now copy real company names and fake HR profiles, and 2026 reports say AI is making fake recruiters and even video interviews more convincing. The rule of thumb that catches most: a genuine employer does not charge you to hire you.

**E2. Expensive "pay after placement" or guaranteed-job courses [Anecdotal]**
Community posts describe multi-lakh-rupee income-share or pay-after-placement programmes targeting tier-3 students, with unclear job quality. I found only anecdotal evidence, so verify any programme's placement claims independently before paying.

**E3. Information overload and coaching commercialisation [Anecdotal]**
Endless roadmaps, YouTube playlists, and paid courses, with no way for a student to know which path fits their college tier, target role, and time remaining.

## 4. Root-cause map

| Visible symptom | Likely root cause | Who can fix it |
|---|---|---|
| Not shortlisted | Resume shows tools, not outcomes; weak or missing keywords; no verifiable projects | Student, mentor |
| Fails aptitude / online test | Late or no practice on reasoning and timed tests | Student, college |
| Fails technical round | Shallow fundamentals; memorised solutions; over-reliance on AI | Student, college |
| Fails HR / behavioural round | Poor self-introduction; no company research; weak communication | Student, mock-interview practice |
| Applies a lot, hears nothing | Mass applying; ghost postings; silent screening; no referrals | Student, employers |
| Passes tests but no offers | Fewer entry slots; high competition at mass recruiters | Market, policy |
| Loses money to fake offers | Desperation plus low verification habits | Student, platforms, regulators |
| Does not know what to prepare | No career guidance, unclear role paths | College, mentors |

## 5. What the evidence does *not* show

- It does not show that "80–90% of Indian engineers are unemployable". That claim traces to old, narrow tests. Current employability scores are around 56% overall and 78–80% for CS/IT, though those tests measure baseline skills and not hiring outcomes.
- It does not show that AI alone is causing the entry-level slowdown. The ICRIER authors themselves attribute part of it to post-pandemic correction.
- I found no reliable India-wide dataset on student mental health during job hunting, or on how many freshers end up in scams. Both are worth tracking in any programme.

## 6. Proposed solution: the Proof-of-Skill Pathway

**Core idea:** replace "prepare everything, hope for the best" with a loop of **Diagnose → Plan → Practise with feedback → Prove → Apply smartly → Learn from outcomes**. Each layer below can run alone, but they work best together.

### 6.1 For students: a practical roadmap

**Year 1–2 (foundations)**
- Take a baseline diagnostic: aptitude, logic, one programming language, English communication. Repeat every semester.
- Pick one language deeply (Python, Java, or C++), then learn Git, SQL, and basic Linux.
- Build the habit: 45–60 minutes a day of problem-solving plus one small project a month.
- Join a coding club or hackathon; write a short technical post about something you learned.

**Year 3 (proof)**
- Learn the core CS subjects that interviews test: data structures and algorithms, DBMS/SQL, OS, computer networks, OOP.
- Solve a *focused* set of problems by pattern (arrays, hashing, two pointers, trees, graphs, DP basics) and be able to explain each solution aloud. Depth beats volume.
- Build **one or two serious projects**: deployed, with a README, tests, and a clear problem statement, ideally with a real user or a real dataset.
- Do at least one internship (AICTE portals, company programmes, or a small startup or open-source contribution).

**Year 4 / final year (conversion)**
- Weekly mock interviews (technical and HR), ideally recorded, with feedback.
- Prepare a 60-second self-introduction and 3 project stories using *problem → your decision → result*.
- Tailor the resume for each role family; apply through campus, off-campus, referrals, and company career pages.
- Keep a tracker: role, company, stage reached, reason for rejection, what to fix.

**Use AI as a tutor, not a substitute.** Ask it to explain, quiz you, and review your code, then re-solve without it. If you cannot explain your own code line by line, do not put it on your resume.

### 6.2 Resume and profile checklist (evidence-informed)

- One page, single column, standard headings, plain fonts, PDF format.
- Bullets, not paragraphs. Lead each bullet with an action and end with a result or scale where possible.
- Keep only: name, professional email, phone, city, LinkedIn, GitHub/portfolio. Drop photos, full addresses, and generic objective statements.
- Projects: problem, stack, your role, outcome, link. Every link must work.
- Mirror the *exact skill names* from the job description where you honestly have them.
- Proofread the email address and phone number; name the file properly (Firstname_Lastname_Resume.pdf).
- Test-parse it: paste the PDF text into a plain editor and check that nothing is scrambled.

### 6.3 Safe job-search rules

1. Never pay to get hired. No fee for registration, training kits, or "background verification".
2. Verify recruiters on the company's official careers page; confirm offer letters by email from a company domain.
3. Do not share Aadhaar, PAN, or original certificates before a verified offer and joining process.
4. Be sceptical of unsolicited WhatsApp or Telegram offers with high pay and no interview.
5. Before joining any paid programme, ask for verifiable outcomes: names, roles, salaries, and the contract's payment terms.
6. Report scams to the cyber-crime portal and the platform.

### 6.4 For colleges

| Action | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Skill diagnostics from Year 1** | Baseline and semester-wise tests; dashboard by branch so weak areas are visible early |
| **Industry-linked electives** | 1–2 electives per year co-taught or reviewed by practitioners (cloud, data, testing, DevOps, applied AI) |
| **Project-based credit** | Substitute part of theory exams with reviewed, deployed projects |
| **Internship credit and matching** | Structured internships from Year 2, with the placement cell tracking outcomes |
| **Mock-interview infrastructure** | Alumni and industry volunteers run technical and HR mocks; written feedback stored per student |
| **Placement cell as career centre** | Explain role paths, funnel stages, and off-campus channels to all years, not just final-years |
| **Transparent outcomes** | Publish placement quality (roles, salaries, share of students placed), not just headline packages |

### 6.5 For employers and recruiters

- Publish role requirements and process timelines; send a status update or rejection within a stated window.
- Use skill-based assessments with a *live explanation* stage rather than relying solely on proctoring.
- Offer paid apprenticeships or structured internships as a hiring route, especially for tier-2/3 candidates.
- Be honest about onboarding timelines; delayed joining hurts students financially and emotionally.
- Take down filled or non-existent postings.

### 6.6 For policy and ecosystem

- Scale verified internship programmes and publish completion and conversion data.
- Maintain a verified-employer registry and a fast reporting channel for fake offers.
- Set disclosure rules for pay-after-placement and income-share programmes (fees, refund terms, verified placement outcomes).
- Fund faculty upskilling and open, regularly updated curriculum modules.

### 6.7 If you want to build this as a product (MVP idea)

**Name (working):** *SkillProof*

| Module | What it does | MVP scope |
|---|---|---|
| **Diagnostic** | 45-minute assessment across aptitude, coding basics, and communication; outputs a skill map and target-role fit | Yes |
| **Personalised plan** | Weekly plan sized to time left (for example 30, 90, or 180 days) and college tier | Yes |
| **Practice engine** | Timed aptitude, pattern-based coding sets, CS-fundamentals quizzes | Yes |
| **AI mock interviewer** | Technical and HR mocks with feedback on clarity, structure, and correctness; follow-up questions to test understanding | Yes |
| **Resume and profile builder** | Parses the resume, scores readability, suggests outcome-based rewrites, flags dead links | Yes |
| **Project portfolio** | Guides one deployed project with milestones and a reviewer checklist; issues a shareable "proof" page | Phase 2 |
| **Verified opportunity feed** | Curated listings from verified employers, with scam filters and posting-age checks | Phase 2 |
| **Mentor marketplace** | Alumni and practitioners for reviews and mocks | Phase 2 |
| **Outcome tracker** | Application tracker with rejection-reason capture; feeds back into the plan | Phase 2 |

**Success metrics:** diagnostic-to-plan completion rate; weekly practice consistency; mock-interview score improvement; interview-call rate per 100 applications; offers per cohort; time to first offer; scam reports avoided.

**Key risks and mitigations**
- *Crowded coaching market:* differentiate on diagnosis, feedback, and verified outcomes rather than content volume.
- *Trust:* publish outcomes honestly, including failures.
- *AI over-reliance and integrity:* design mocks and practice to require explanation, not just answers.
- *Access:* offer mobile-first, low-data, and regional-language support; partner with colleges so tier-2/3 students are not priced out.
- *Data privacy:* minimal collection, clear consent, no selling student data.

## 7. Suggested 90-day crash plan for a final-year student

| Weeks | Focus | Deliverables |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Diagnose and target | Baseline test; choose 1–2 role families; rewrite resume; fix LinkedIn and GitHub |
| 3–6 | Fundamentals | 5 problem patterns mastered; SQL and DBMS revision; OS/CN essentials; daily aptitude drills |
| 7–9 | Proof | Finish or polish one project (deploy, README, tests); prepare 3 project stories |
| 10–12 | Interview conversion | 2 mock interviews a week; targeted applications (10–15 per week to matched roles); referrals; tracker review every Sunday |
